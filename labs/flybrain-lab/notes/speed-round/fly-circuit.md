## Numbers

- Cells: 5,177 KC, 96 MBON, 323 DAN (307 PAM, 16 PPL1), 2 APL, 2 DPM. 346 cells feed the calyx (projection neurons of every modality); each KC samples 4.7 of them on average (median 5, 10-90 % 2-7, counting partners with 3+ synapses); each input cell reaches 54-66 KCs.
- The plastic surface: 38,915 (KC, MBON) synapse pairs, 169,931 synapses, over 77 MBON cells (median 138 KCs per MBON cell of about 2,600 per side): on the order of 20,000 plastic "slots" per hemisphere. The other 132,000 cells are fixed wiring. The fly is weights-big and memory-small; the lab's table is 25 times the weights.
- KC outputs: 42 % to MBONs, 29 % to APL, 22 % to DPM, 3 % to DANs, 3 % to other KCs. KC inputs: 75 % calyx (projection neurons), 21 % APL. The KC-APL-KC loop (117k and 99k synapses) is the sparsifier.
- MBON inputs: 62 % KC, 24 % rest of the brain, 7 % other MBONs, 5 % DAN. MBON outputs: 82 % to the rest of the brain, 12 % to other MBONs, 3.5 % to DANs. Seven MBON types (15, 19, 20, 25, 29, 33, 35) take under 10 % of their input from KCs: they read other readouts.
- DAN inputs: 60 % rest of the brain, 24 % KC, 10.5 % MBON (feedback). PAM07 and PAM13 take 49-57 % of their input from MBONs; PAM09 takes 91 % from KCs and sends 69 % of its output back onto KCs. DAN outputs: 61 % onto MBONs, 10 % APL, 8 % KC.

## What the table lacks, by the circuit

1. **A computed write signal.** The fly writes a KC-MBON synapse only when KC activity meets dopamine, and the dopamine cells are driven by the brain's state, by the key itself and by the readout's feedback (prediction error). The table writes every (context, next byte) it sees with one rule, which is why extending it evicted as much as it added (probe 1). Gate: write where the model's own prediction was wrong. Test: `checks/loss_pass.py` + `checks/gated_tables.py` (results under "Write what surprised you" in SPEED.md).
2. **Compartments.** Fifteen memories with their own rates and decays read the same key in parallel; second-order MBONs read the readouts. A project tier written freely and a corpus tier that admits entries confirmed by repetition would keep probe 1's gain without its loss. Untested.
3. **The key is a sparse random code of the state.** 346 channels expand to 2,600 KCs at 5 claws each and APL keeps about 5 % active; neighbouring inputs share part of the code, so a memory written for one is read by the others. The table's key is an exact hash of bytes. A table keyed by the model's hidden state through the lab's frozen sparse expansion (top-k of 4,096) is the fly's mechanism at corpus scale. Untested; the largest of the three.
4. **Consolidation** (DPM, sleep): memories move to a stable form offline. The counterpart here would be distilling what the table keeps answering into the weights, the reverse of growing tables. Not pursued.

What each read changes about the test:

**1. The key.** FlyHash (Dasgupta 2017) is a frozen sparse-*binary* expansion of a normalised input (the fly: 50 channels to 2,000 KCs, about 6 inputs per KC; the benchmarks: m = 10 d, 10 % of inputs per unit) followed by a fixed top-k (the fly's 5 %), and it preserves l2 neighbourhoods only; its gain over LSH is largest at k <= 8 and fades by k = 64. Litwin-Kumar 2017: a frozen random expansion with small in-degree (K* = 7 at N = 50, M = 2,000) is near-optimal when the inputs and the expansion are normalised; learned input weights pay only at K > 10, and expansion beyond 10-50x adds nothing. The readout is the mean of the k active slots (Dasgupta 2018: novelty vs shared slots r = -0.94). Generalisation and interference are the same channel: two "different" odours share 30-33 % of their KCs and the unpaired one lost 27 % of what the paired one lost (80 %), linear in overlap across four odours (r = 0.90; Hige 2015); Lin 2014: sparseness matters only as decorrelation, and a broader, more correlated code breaks the discrimination of *similar* odours only. No paper uses a similarity-preserving address for a count table or memory inside a sequence model (one search; none found). So for the test: key = frozen sparse-binary expansion of a normalised context vector, fixed top-k, address = the winners, readout = the mean of the k slots' counts; "near" is set by the input encoding (a positional one-hot of the last n bytes gives substitutions, not shifts), which no source covers; sweep k and m on held-out bits/byte, since the fly's 5 % and the benchmarks' settings do not transfer; keep the exact table as the control and measure the overlap between contexts that need different next bytes, which is where the fly's code fails too.

**2. Compartments.** Parallel, not promotion: Aso & Rubin 2016 find the data "consistent with a model in which memory formation and consolidation can occur independently and in parallel in individual MB compartments" and call fast-to-slow conversion "an open question"; Aso 2012 says the traces form "in parallel" and interact. The compartments differ in write rule, not only decay: PPL1-gamma1pedc holds the strongest immediate memory for minutes to hours with a capacity of one (the newer odour overwrites) and forms no 4-day memory even after ten spaced trainings; PAM-alpha1 holds about a day, keeps at least two associations, and resists erasure; a PPL1 line with "barely detectable" immediate memory forms 1- and 4-day memory after ten trainings at 15-minute rests. Stabilisation is in place (spaced-trained memory resists a DAN pulse that erases single-trained memory), readout is a signed sum of the compartments (Aso 2014b), and forgetting is active, by the same dopamine cells through another receptor (Berry 2012). So for the test: parallel tiers on one key, each with its own write rule (overwrite vs retain; admission to the slow tier gated by repetition, which is the fly's spaced training) and its own decay, read as a sum; the "promotion" in the first TODO line was my invention, not the fly's.

**3. Consolidation.** The fly strengthens the slow store in place rather than copying from the fast one: alpha/beta neurons are needed for retrieval from 15 minutes and carry all of the 24-hour memory, while alpha'/beta' and gamma are needed only to 1.5-9 hours and then dispensable (Cervantes-Sandoval 2013; the copy the authors call "system consolidation" is their hypothesis, untested). The long form needs spacing, not repetition (five cycles at 15-minute rests; five massed cycles give only the protein-synthesis-independent form), and an energy gate that is "necessary and sufficient" but a switch, not an amplifier (Placais 2017: opening it gives LTM after two cycles and adds nothing after five); sleep protects memory by blocking dopamine-driven forgetting (Berry 2015). Complementary learning systems (Kumaran 2016, McClelland 1995): the slow learner must interleave new with old at a small learning rate or it interferes; schema-consistent content consolidates in hours to days; what the fast store loses before consolidation is lost. The machine-learning precedents settle the first question: RETRO trained with retrieval and run without it scores exactly its no-retrieval baseline (C4 0.78 vs 0.66 bits/byte with it, at 7.5 B), so co-training with a memory leaves no gain in the weights by default; the one worked example of moving a memory into parameters is MLP Memory (arXiv 2508.01832: an MLP trained to output the kNN-LM distribution, 0.4 KL + 0.6 CE, WikiText-103 perplexity 15.81 to 11.41 with no datastore at inference); no paper fine-tunes a model to drop its memory. This matches the probe of the same morning (`SPEED.md`, "Where the knowledge lives"): our table-trained models hold almost nothing in their weights. So the test needs explicit pressure: withdraw the table during training (constant or ramped: `--mem-dropout`, running as `checks/cons_gpu1.sh`) or distil its distribution as a target, scheduled, and measured by removal.
